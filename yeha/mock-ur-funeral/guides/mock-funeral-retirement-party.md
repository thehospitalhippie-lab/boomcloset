Most retirement parties run on autopilot: sheet cake in the break room, a card everyone signed in a hurry,
a boss who says "big shoes to fill," and a gift card. Then the person who gave decades to the place walks
out to the parking lot, and that's that.

A Mock (ur) Funeral gives the goodbye some weight. The retiree isn't going anywhere. Their job title is.
And the people who worked beside them finally get to say what they'd usually save for a eulogy, while the
retiree is sitting right there to hear it.

## What it actually is

A Mock (ur) Funeral is a facilitated, three-act ceremony from Golden Soul Circus, hosted by hospice nurses
Brad and Cass Burns. It's made for anyone "ready to say goodbye to an old version of themselves." For a
retiree, that old version has a name badge, a parking spot and an inbox.

- **Act I, the Toast.** "Give your flowers while you live." Coworkers go around the room and name a quality
  in the retiree they might never have said out loud at work.
- **Act II, the Roast.** "Hold cold feet to the fire." The honest, loving ribbing from people who sat through
  every one of their meetings and still like them.
- **Act III, the Prost.** "Rip sleeves off grief." Saying the last thing, while there's still time, before
  everyone scatters to different desks and different lives.

It takes about an hour: 15 minutes to set up and a 45-minute show.

## Why it fits a retirement

Retirement is a real ending, even when it's a happy one. People who've shared a workplace for years often
never say the important stuff, because work isn't where you say it. The ceremony makes room for it.

It also gives the party a centerpiece. Instead of a speech from one person, everyone gets a turn: the
mentor, the work spouse, the new hire they trained, the person who always borrowed their stapler. The
retiree hears what their working life meant to the people in it, and gets to answer back.

And it's a little ridiculous, on purpose. You're holding a funeral for a job title. The ceremony is
"equal parts sacred and silly," so there's room for laughing and for the lump in the throat.

## Nobody has to perform

Workplaces are full of people who'd rather not speak in front of a crowd. That's fine. Each guest picks how
far in they go on the day:

- **All in:** speak, be toasted and roasted, join every act.
- **No engagement:** be there and listen. You won't be called on.
- **No interaction:** witness only. No one will address you or speak about you.

The retiree can choose a level too, and you can tell the hosts ahead of time what's off-limits. Office
politics, the reorg, the thing that happened at the 2019 holiday party: say the word and it stays out.

## Where it can happen

In someone's home, at a venue or facility, or as a community workshop. If your team is renting a room for
the send-off, the ceremony slots in as the main event, with food and mingling around it.

## Tips for the planner

- **Tell the retiree, or don't.** Some people love a surprise. Others want time to think about what they're
  leaving behind. You know your person.
- **Send Find Your Words early.** Coworkers write better toasts with a few days' notice than with five
  minutes in the hallway.
- **Invite the people who matter, not just the org chart.** Former teammates and folks who already retired
  often have the best stories.

## One honest note

This is ceremony, not therapy. For the hour, Brad and Cass are your hosts, not your nurses. They hold
space for saying goodbye to an old self. They don't diagnose, counsel or treat.
